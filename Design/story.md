# Story

The narrative spine. Level-by-level breakdown is in `levels.md`,
systems are in `mechanics.md`, one-page summary in `overview.md`.

---

## Premise

A man who has worked the same corporate job for fifteen years finally
resigns. It's accepted in ninety seconds. Then he discovers his badge
was deactivated the moment he signed — and every door in the building,
including the way out, reads a badge.

He has to be four hours away by six o'clock. He is standing inside a
building that no longer has any record of him.

## The Cold Open — the party

Playable, four minutes. Not a cutscene; the player has to do this
themselves.

A rented function room, cheap decorations, music too quiet to enjoy.
The player can wander and talk to coworkers. Nobody says anything that
matters.

1. **The award.** Fifteen years of service. The plaque is misspelled.
   The manager reads his job title off a card and gets that wrong too.
   Polite applause.
2. **The new hire.** Twenty-three, three weeks in, seated at the same
   table. Describes the job as temporary — *"couple years, save up,
   then I'll actually do the thing."* Almost word for word what he said
   fifteen years ago. There is no dialogue option to warn them.
3. **The call.** His phone buzzes mid-conversation. **Mom.** Answering
   means standing up and walking out of a conversation while people are
   watching. Most players will let it ring. That is the design.

   If the player *does* answer, she says nothing important — *"just
   calling to say hi."* It changes nothing that happens. It changes
   everything about how the next three hours feel.

Hard cut. Title card: three days. **She died that night.**

## The morning

The funeral is today, 6:00 PM, four hours' drive away. He wakes at
7:12, prints his resignation, and goes in to quit before he drives out.
He has all day, and he's going in first because he is not coming back.

This is narrative weight, not a timer — the game never counts it down
or asks the player to wait. See `mechanics.md`.

## The resignation

He arrives at 8:50 and hands the letter to his manager at 9:05.

There is no obstruction. Halloran reads it, isn't surprised, isn't
angry, isn't moved. He signs it. *"Fifteen years. That's a long time.
Good for you."* Ninety seconds and it's done.

The player gets a real, earned moment of relief. The game appears to
be over.

## The trap

Access is terminated effective immediately — standard offboarding.
Nobody takes his badge. It's still in his hand. It's just void.

The elevator needs an active badge to select a floor. The stairwells
are badge-locked. Everything in this building has always been
badge-locked; he never noticed, because his badge always worked.

He is not being held prisoner. Nobody is stopping him. He has simply
been deleted from the system while still standing inside it.

## The horror — erasure

The building doesn't attack him. It stops registering him, and the
longer he's unregistered the less he's there:

- Coworkers he's known a decade look up, don't register him, go back
  to their monitors. Not cruelty — he isn't in the system, so he isn't
  there.
- His reflection in the glass partitions lags, then stops appearing.
- The HUD degrades — crosshair, then prompts, then subtitles.
- The inner monologue drifts from *"I need to get out"* to *"he needs
  to get out."*

The people still working are the ones who exist. He's the one who
stopped. That inverts the resentment the whole opening built.

Nothing supernatural is ever confirmed. Everything stays legible as
burnout and dissociation, and it's worse that way — the player can't
file it under "ghosts" and stop recognising their own job in it.

## The doubt

The real lock isn't the turnstile. Approaching any exit escalates an
internal monologue that does arithmetic at him — rent, savings, fifteen
years of experience in something nobody outside this building does.
Nothing ever blocks the player. The game just makes them feel why they
won't.

> *"Fifteen years. If you're not this, what are you?"*
> *"Nobody out there is waiting for you. That's why you stayed."*

## The suit

Halloran doesn't chase anyone. He signed the form, said his one line,
went back to his monitor — that indifference is the point, and it
doesn't change below Floor 9.

What does chase him is never named, never given a face, and never
speaks. A suit and tie, nothing where the face should be. The only
setup it gets is a phone call: as he leaves Halloran's office on Floor
10, glimpsed through the glass, Halloran is already dialing an
extension, talking too quietly to hear. Nothing explains it. It's the
last time Halloran matters to the plot.

Below Floor 9 the suit is glimpsed before it's a threat — motionless,
facing away, gone if you look away and back — and starts actively
hunting on Floor 7, once he's somewhere he isn't supposed to be. Being
caught doesn't end the game. It re-files him: he wakes up back where he
started the floor, and something he'd already found is sealed again.
Caught means processed back into the system, not erased out of it.

The suit is never explained and never needs to be. It reads equally as
what a company actually does to someone who tries to leave, or as
something the building manifests once he stops being legible to it. The
game doesn't pick.

## The thesis

The lobby turnstiles read badges **on the way in.** Going out, they
never needed one. They never did. In fifteen years he never once tried
to leave without permission.

He walks out. Nobody stops him. Nobody ever would have.

## Endings

- **Walk out.** The real ending. The door opens. It always did. Whether
  he makes the funeral is the question the player carries.
- **The secret one.** A player who walks to the lobby doors early and
  tries them gets out. Ninety seconds long. Nobody stops them.

## Still Open

- What the thing he gave up actually is (camera / guitar / knife roll —
  needs to be one concrete object, dressed through the apartment)
- Whether the surveillance-metrics thread (yield curves, blink rate,
  projected replacement dates) is found in the world, and on which
  floors
- Whether there's a predecessor — someone who tried this before and
  left a trail of routes and notes through the building
- An ending where he takes an offer to stay, if there's a scene that
  earns it
